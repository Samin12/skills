# Pink-Sheet Visual Thinking Framework

Source lens: Think by Matt Church and Peter Cook (2018), which describes the pink sheet as a compact intellectual-property snapshot. This reference is a distilled operating guide, not a reproduction of the book.

## Core Frame

A pink sheet turns one idea into five aligned elements:

- Model: logical context, usually a diagram at the top left.
- Metaphor: emotional context, usually a spoken or imageable comparison at the top right.
- Point: the central claim, phrase, mantra, or A/B statement.
- Case study: logical content that proves practical application.
- Story: emotional content that makes the idea memorable.

Use it to move from raw material to reusable communication. The goal is not to decorate the idea; the goal is to think it through across the full spectrum.

## Full-Spectrum Thinking

Design every visual across two axes:

- Logic to emotion: evidence/model on one side, story/metaphor on the other.
- Concrete to abstract: content at the bottom, concept in the middle, context at the top.

When an input is too abstract, move down into examples, scenes, data, and steps. When it is too detailed, move up into category, pattern, principle, and model.

## Point

One point gets one pink sheet. Keep points declarative rather than question-shaped.

Useful point-making passes:

- A/B: A is the sharp title; B is the clarifying subtitle.
- Mantra: a repeatable phrase people can carry away.
- Left/right: write one precise/logical version and one vivid/emotional version.
- 7/17/37/70: explain it for a child, a teen, a busy adult, and a wise elder; harvest the best language.

For visuals, the A statement usually becomes the main headline or center label. The B statement becomes supporting copy, tooltip copy, or a reveal panel.

## Models

Models are geometric thinking tools. Choose simple shapes first:

- Ladder or staircase: best for why, value, progress, maturity, aspiration, status, or priority.
- Venn or overlapping circles: best for how, relationships, strategic territory, intersections, and shared conditions.
- Matrix or grid: best for what, choices, tactical stages, categories, tradeoffs, and pathways.
- Contrast frame: best for if, before/after, old/new, fixed/growth, risk/opportunity, or transformation.
- Cycle, loop, or flywheel: best for repeated behavior, feedback, compounding, habits, or systems.
- Map or timeline: best for journey, sequence, branching decisions, and narrative movement.

Layer models in three reveals:

1. Awareness: name the main parts.
2. Distinctions: name intersections, boundaries, and contrasts.
3. Prescriptions: show actions, choices, or next moves.

Work the intersections. Many strong ideas live on boundary lines, overlaps, transitions, and exceptions.

## Metaphors

Metaphors create a fast mental picture. They should land the point, not carry all the explanation.

Sources:

- Expanded cliche: familiar, easy to grasp, but needs ownership and specificity.
- Icon-inspired: based on widely recognized symbols, but do not rely on clip art.
- Personal-interest: drawn from the user's hobbies, background, or voice.
- Perfectly aligned: the metaphor maps cleanly onto the model and the point.

Test alignment by saying the point, explaining the model, naming the metaphor, and repeating the point. If the loop feels strained, change the metaphor.

For generated images, the metaphor is often the best visual seed. For HTML, the model is usually the best structural seed.

## Case Studies

Case studies make the idea practical. Shape them as incident, point, benefit.

Useful ingredients:

- pictures or concrete scene details
- numbers, measurements, comparisons, or percentages
- process steps the audience can reuse
- audience-specific industry examples

In an HTML widget, case studies work well as toggles, data callouts, process cards, sliders, or before/after panels.

## Stories

Stories make the idea memorable. The pink sheet should store the story title and key beats, not the entire transcript.

Story types:

- Icon story: someone admired; useful when the user should not be the hero.
- Personal story: use humility and accessibility.
- Anecdotal story: everyday observation made meaningful.
- Historical story: safe distance, double lesson, broader credibility.

Story tools:

- drama: scene, character, stakes, tension
- humor: situational recognition rather than forced jokes
- social objects: surprising or sticky details people can repeat

In an HTML widget, stories work well as step-through scenes, timeline beats, or flip cards. In an image, they work well as a single decisive moment.

## Pink Sheet 2.0

Add these when the user needs a richer reusable body of work:

- Word: the organizing theme or folder label.
- Rank: Ace, face card, or supporting card in the idea deck.
- Quote: attributed external reinforcement.
- Learning activity: what a group should do to internalize the idea.
- Infographic: data rendered visually.
- Questions: coaching or facilitation prompts that walk through the model.

## Delivery Modes

Match the output to how the user will use it:

- Speaker or author: tell through content, stories, examples, and proof.
- Trainer or mentor: show the concept and keep people on the point.
- Facilitator or coach: ask through context, models, and questions.

For a script fragment, decide whether the fragment wants to be spoken, taught, coached, sold, or explored. Let that drive the widget interaction.

## Artifact Pairing

For every request, create two complementary artifacts:

- HTML widget: precise structure, labels, relationships, interactions, evidence, and reusable thinking.
- Generated image: emotional mnemonic, metaphor, mood, hero scene, or visual shorthand.

The HTML artifact should usually be more exact. The image artifact should usually be more memorable.

## Fast Checklist

- Is there exactly one central point?
- Does the model show the bigger picture?
- Does the metaphor land the point without over-explaining it?
- Is there practical proof or an example?
- Is there a human scene, emotional beat, or story hook?
- Does the HTML invite the user to explore?
- Does the generated image make the idea easier to remember?
